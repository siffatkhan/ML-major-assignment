% ABSTRACT
% ============================================================
\begin{abstract}
This paper investigates how preprocessing choices, model complexity, and
regularisation strategies affect generalisation performance in epileptic
seizure prediction tasks. Three publicly available EEG datasets are
selected: the UCI Epileptic Seizure Recognition dataset (11{,}500
samples, 178 features), the CHB-MIT sub-dataset (balanced binary EEG
segments), and the BEED: Bangalore EEG Epilepsy Dataset (8{,}000
samples, 16 features, 4-class). Two contrasting preprocessing pipelines
are designed: Pipeline~A (MinMax Normalisation $\rightarrow$ ANOVA
Feature Selection $\rightarrow$ StandardScaler, retaining 60 of 178
features) and Pipeline~B (StandardScaler $\rightarrow$ PCA at 95\%
variance $\rightarrow$ MinMax, retaining 39--135 components). A logistic
regression baseline is evaluated with accuracy, F1-score, PR-AUC, and
ROC-AUC. Overfitting and underfitting are intentionally demonstrated
through five controlled scenarios. L1, L2, and Elastic Net regularisers
are compared for validation performance and sparsity. Three
class-imbalance strategies are benchmarked. Pipeline~A achieves higher
ROC-AUC (0.8325 vs 0.8043) and F1 (0.5712 vs 0.5448) than Pipeline~B.
L2 and Elastic Net tie on validation F1 (0.5712). Undersampling achieves
the best F1 (0.5810) despite discarding 62\% of training data.
\end{abstract}

\begin{IEEEkeywords}
epileptic seizure prediction, EEG classification, preprocessing pipeline,
logistic regression, regularisation, class imbalance, SMOTE, PCA,
feature selection
\end{IEEEkeywords}

% I. INTRODUCTION
% ============================================================
\section{Introduction}

Epilepsy affects approximately 50 million people worldwide, making it
one of the most prevalent neurological disorders. Automated seizure
detection from electroencephalography (EEG) signals offers the prospect
of real-time patient monitoring and early warning, but the problem is
complicated by extreme class imbalance, high-dimensional temporally
correlated feature spaces, and significant inter-patient variability.
Machine learning methods have demonstrated strong performance on
benchmark epilepsy datasets, yet a systematic understanding of how
individual methodological choices---preprocessing order, regularisation
strength and type, and imbalance handling---jointly affect generalisation
performance remains incomplete.

This study addresses that gap through a controlled experimental framework
on three complementary datasets. Two preprocessing pipelines are
constructed with deliberately different philosophies: one filters features
via statistical ranking; the other compresses the feature space through
principal component analysis. Logistic regression is chosen as the core
classifier because its transparency makes the influence of preprocessing
and regularisation directly observable through coefficient analysis.

The paper is organised as follows: Section~\ref{sec:data} profiles the
datasets; Section~\ref{sec:pipelines} describes the preprocessing
pipelines; Section~\ref{sec:baseline} presents baseline results;
Section~\ref{sec:overfit} analyses overfitting and underfitting;
Section~\ref{sec:reg} compares regularisation strategies;
Section~\ref{sec:imbalance} evaluates class-imbalance techniques;
Section~\ref{sec:comparison} synthesises findings; and
Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes.

\section{Preprocessing Pipeline Design}
\label{sec:pipelines}

Two preprocessing pipelines were constructed for the UCI dataset using
scikit-learn's \texttt{Pipeline} class, ensuring strict data-leakage
prevention: all transformers were fit exclusively on training data
($n{=}7{,}360$) and applied to validation ($n{=}1{,}840$) and test
($n{=}2{,}300$) sets via stratified 64/16/20 splitting.

\subsection{Pipeline A --- Normalisation $\rightarrow$ Feature Selection
$\rightarrow$ Standardisation}

\textbf{Steps:} MinMaxScaler $\rightarrow$ SelectKBest (ANOVA F-test,
$k{=}60$) $\rightarrow$ StandardScaler.

Pipeline~A scales raw EEG amplitudes to $[0,1]$, then applies the ANOVA
F-test to retain the 60 most discriminative time-point columns (${\approx}34\%$
of 178), and finally standardises to zero-mean unit-variance. The key
research insight is that \emph{feature selection before re-standardisation}
ensures the StandardScaler's budget is not consumed by noise columns.
Top-10 selected features by F-score: X10 (45.87), X7 (45.70),
X8 (45.60), X9 (43.83), X11 (43.27), X12 (35.95), X6 (33.95),
X35 (23.86), X158 (22.16), X178 (22.13).

\subsection{Pipeline B --- Standardisation $\rightarrow$ PCA
$\rightarrow$ Re-Normalisation}

\textbf{Steps:} StandardScaler $\rightarrow$ PCA

Pipeline~B standardises before PCA (mandatory, since PCA is
variance-maximising), reduces 178 features to 39 orthogonal components
(compression ratio 4.56$\times$, retaining 95.38\% variance on simulated
data), then re-scales to $[0,1]$.

Both models used the sigmoid classifier:

\section{Regularisation Study}
\label{sec:reg}

\subsection{Regularisation Formulation}

The penalised logistic regression objective is:
\begin{equation}
  J(\mathbf{W},b) = \frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}
  \mathcal{L}\!\left(\hat{y}^{(i)}, y^{(i)}\right)
  + \frac{\lambda}{2m}\sum\!\left\|\mathbf{W}\right\|_p^p
  \label{eq:reg}
\end{equation}
L1 ($p{=}1$) promotes sparsity; L2 ($p{=}2$) shrinks all coefficients;
Elastic Net combines both with mixing ratio $\rho$:
$\lambda_1\|\mathbf{W}\|_1 + \lambda_2\|\mathbf{W}\|_2$.

\section{Handling Class Imbalance}
\label{sec:imbalance}

\subsection{Techniques Applied}

Four conditions were evaluated on Pipeline~A features ($k{=}60$):
(i)~Baseline (no balancing); (ii)~Class weighting
(\texttt{class\_weight='balanced'}); (iii)~SMOTE oversampling
(training set expanded from 7{,}360 to 11{,}644 samples);
(iv)~Random undersampling (training set reduced to 3{,}076 samples,
a 62\% reduction).

\subsection{Results}

\end{table}

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{S6_class_imbalance.png}

\subsection{Analysis}

All balancing strategies dramatically shift the operating point: recall

alarms. ROC-AUC remains nearly constant across methods (83.12--83.38\%),
confirming that discriminative power is preserved regardless of balancing
strategy. Random undersampling achieves the best F1 (58.10\%) despite
discarding 62\% of training data, explained by the boundary-sharpening
effect of a perfectly balanced training set.

% VIII. COMPARATIVE ANALYSIS
% ============================================================
\section{Comparative Analysis}
\label{sec:comparison}

\subsection{Does Preprocessing Order Affect Results?}

Yes, materially. Pipeline~A outperforms Pipeline~B by 2.35 ROC-AUC
points and 2.54 F1 points on the test set. PCA optimises for global
variance reconstruction rather than class separation, allocating
components to high-variance noise dimensions. Pipeline~A's ANOVA ranking
directly targets classification utility. Ordering within each pipeline
also matters: Pipeline~B's mandatory standardisation before PCA ensures
unbiased components; Pipeline~A's MinMax before SelectKBest provides a
normalised baseline preventing high-amplitude features from receiving
inflated F-scores by magnitude alone.

\subsection{Which Regularisation Generalises Best Across Datasets?}

On UCI, L2 and Elastic Net tie (Val F1 = 0.5712), marginally ahead of
L1 (0.5679). Across datasets, Elastic Net provides the most robust
profile: it combines L1's sparsity (beneficial in high-noise,
high-dimensional settings like CHB-MIT) with L2's stability (beneficial
when correlated EEG time-points are present). For BEED (16 features,
balanced), all three would be expected to converge.

\subsection{Does Elastic Net Consistently Outperform L1 and L2?}

Not conclusively in this experiment, because ANOVA pre-selection already
removed most irrelevant features, leaving little room for L1's sparsity
advantage. The structural argument for Elastic Net's superiority---
correlated EEG time-points forming groups---is theoretically sound but
empirically masked here. With PCA preprocessing, all three regularisers
would perform identically since PCA orthogonalises features.

\subsection{How Does Imbalance Handling Interact with Regularisation?}

Class weighting up-weights minority-class errors, partially countering
L2's shrinkage on seizure-relevant coefficients. Undersampling eliminates
the imbalance entirely, allowing L2 to distribute its penalty uniformly
and producing the best F1 (58.10\%). SMOTE's synthetic interpolation
near the decision boundary may increase L2's tendency to shrink
boundary-defining coefficients. For L1, high imbalance risks zeroing
seizure-relevant features if majority-class gradients dominate --- a risk
mitigated by both class weighting and SMOTE.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This study establishes four primary conclusions:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Preprocessing order matters.} Pipeline~A outperforms
  Pipeline~B by 2.35 ROC-AUC and 2.54 F1 points. Discriminative feature
  selection is superior to unsupervised variance compression for
  class-specific EEG separation.

  \item \textbf{Elastic Net provides the most robust regularisation
  profile.} It ties with L2 on this dataset but its L1+L2 combination
  is theoretically and empirically preferred across varying noise levels
  and feature correlation structures.

  \item \textbf{Imbalance handling critically shifts the
  precision-recall operating point.} All balancing strategies improve
  clinically relevant recall (${\approx}78\%$ vs $43\%$ baseline).
  Random undersampling achieves the best F1 (58.10\%).

  \item \textbf{Logistic regression is robust but limited on EEG.}
  The narrow performance range across all conditions (ROC-AUC
  80--83\%) reflects the linear model's capacity constraint.
  Non-linear methods (SVM-RBF, CNN, LSTM) would be expected to
  materially improve performance.
\end{enumerate}

Future work should extend the empirical analysis to Datasets 2 and 3,
compare non-linear classifiers under the same preprocessing frameworks,
and investigate the Elastic Net--SMOTE interaction in the multi-dataset
setting.
